# Historical Syria Explorer: Project Description

**What it is.** Historical Syria Explorer is a free, open-source website that lets anyone, on any phone and without any account, see Syrian cities as they looked in the 1960s and 1970s. It uses declassified American reconnaissance satellite film (KH-7 GAMBIT, KH-9 HEXAGON, CORONA) and compares it directly, street by street, with today's satellite imagery. Seven cities are already live at full film resolution, including Hama, Aleppo, Homs, and Damascus.

**Why it matters.** Syria has lost an immense part of its built heritage to war, destruction, and unplanned reconstruction. For millions of displaced Syrians, entire neighborhoods now exist only in memory. The declassified film archive is the only systematic photographic record of Syrian cities before the transformations of the last fifty years. It shows the historic urban fabric of Homs and Aleppo intact, villages that no longer exist, and the Euphrates valley before the lake. This project turns that raw, hard-to-use archive into something a teacher, a student, a researcher, or a grandmother in the diaspora can open in one tap. It is a tool of cultural memory, education, and research: permanently free, with no ads and no commercial use.

**What has been achieved.** A complete automated pipeline transforms raw archive film into precisely positioned, losslessly sharp map layers. A hand-alignment tool lets human eyes calibrate each frame against the modern landscape. The entire system runs on free public infrastructure at a cost of under one dollar per month. The full Syrian archive of more than 8,500 film frames is cataloged and ready for processing.

**What comes next.** The immediate goals are to complete the alignment and publication of the existing catalog, add an Arabic interface, and support offline use inside Syria. The strategic goal is partnership with satellite imagery providers in Europe and the USA (Maxar, Planet, Airbus, ESA, and national archives) to obtain educational access to additional historical and modern imagery of Syria, so that the record of every city and every era becomes as complete as possible. A modest team of three people (a geospatial developer, a remote-sensing specialist, and an Arabic-speaking outreach coordinator) could, in one year, take this from seven cities to the whole country.
